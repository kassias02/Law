Look up a lawyer in the **state bar** directory where they say they are licensed, using their name and bar number, and confirm they can practice now. If they are not a lawyer, only an [accredited representative](https://www.justice.gov/eoir/can-someone-represent-you-eoir) of an EOIR-recognized organization may represent you in immigration matters, and only through that organization. Also check EOIR's [list of currently disciplined practitioners](https://www.justice.gov/eoir/list-of-currently-disciplined-practitioners).

## How do I verify a lawyer with their state bar?

Always ask for the **full name** as it appears on the license and the **bar number**. Use those details in the official bar or court directory of the state where the person claims to be licensed. Do not rely only on a business card, a website or a social-media ad.

Official directories in the most populous states include:

- **California:** The State Bar of California [Attorney Search](https://apps.calbar.ca.gov/attorney/LicenseeSearch/QuickSearch).
- **Texas:** the State Bar of Texas [Find A Lawyer](https://www.texasbar.com/FindALawyer).
- **Florida:** The Florida Bar [Find a Florida Bar Lawyer](https://www.floridabar.org/directories/find-mbr/).
- **New York:** the New York State Unified Court System [Attorney Search](https://iapps.courts.state.ny.us/attorneyservices/search) (the official directory of registered attorneys).
- **Illinois:** the Attorney Registration and Disciplinary Commission (ARDC) [Lawyer Search](https://www.iardc.org/Lawyer/Search).

If the state is a different one, look up that state's official bar or court directory. EOIR's page [Can Someone Represent You Before EOIR?](https://www.justice.gov/eoir/can-someone-represent-you-eoir) also explains who may represent you.

When you search, check that the name matches, and note the license status, the city and any discipline history shown on the profile. If the person does not appear, do not assume it is only a spelling problem: ask again for the bar number and the state of licensure, or do not hire them.

## What does "good standing" mean?

There is no single federal label. Each bar or licensing authority uses its own wording. What you need to see is whether the person is **currently authorized to practice** in that state, not only whether they were once admitted.

Examples from what those authorities publish:

- On The Florida Bar, a profile may show whether the member is *in good standing* and *eligible to practice law in Florida*.
- In California, the official search displays license status; the State Bar also describes situations in which a licensee is *not eligible to practice*.
- In New York, the official attorney directory lets you look up registration; a *certificate of good standing* is a formal court document, not a substitute for that search.

If the directory says the person is **suspended**, **disbarred** or not authorized to practice, do not hire them for your case. If the status is unclear, call the bar or ask for a written explanation before you sign anything. Being in good standing in one state does not, by itself, mean the person is licensed in every other state.

## Who else may represent me besides a lawyer?

EOIR explains who may appear before an immigration court or the Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA). Besides a licensed attorney, an **accredited representative** may do so, but only under the Recognition & Accreditation program.

According to EOIR's page [Can Someone Represent You Before EOIR?](https://www.justice.gov/eoir/can-someone-represent-you-eoir):

- EOIR accredits an individual who works for a **recognized organization**.
- The accredited representative **may practice immigration law only through that organization**. They cannot take the accreditation with them if they leave, or practice on their own. EOIR describes that as the unauthorized practice of law.
- A **fully accredited** representative may appear before an immigration court or the BIA. A **partially accredited** representative may appear only before USCIS.

Official rosters are posted on EOIR's [R&A program page](https://www.justice.gov/eoir/recognition-accreditation-roster-reports):

- [List of Accredited Representatives](https://www.justice.gov/eoir/page/file/942311/dl) (PDF).
- [List of Recognized Organizations](https://www.justice.gov/eoir/page/file/942301/dl) (PDF).

EOIR notes that contact information on those rosters is provided by each organization, which must report changes. The regulations for the program are at [8 C.F.R. Part 1292](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-8/chapter-V/subchapter-B/part-1292).

If you cannot afford a lawyer, EOIR also publishes a [list of pro bono legal service providers](https://www.justice.gov/eoir/list-pro-bono-legal-service-providers), organized by state. You can [talk to an immigration lawyer](/en/contact/) through this site; this site is not a law firm, and you are not obligated to hire anyone.

## How do I know whether EOIR has disciplined a lawyer or a representative?

Even after you check the state bar, EOIR runs its own [attorney discipline program](https://www.justice.gov/eoir/attorney-discipline-program) for lawyers and representatives who practice before the immigration courts and the BIA.

Check the [list of currently disciplined practitioners](https://www.justice.gov/eoir/list-of-currently-disciplined-practitioners). EOIR also publishes a [list of previously disciplined practitioners](https://www.justice.gov/eoir/list-of-previously-disciplined-practitioners). A person can be licensed in a state and, at the same time, suspended or disbarred before EOIR: it is worth checking both.

## What are the red flags of a scam or a "notario"?

USCIS explains, in [Legal Services](https://www.uscis.gov/scams-fraud-and-misconduct/avoid-scams/legal-services) and [Avoid Scams](https://www.uscis.gov/avoid-scams), that in some countries a "notario público" is an attorney, but **in the United States a notary public is not a lawyer and cannot represent you** in immigration matters. Be cautious if someone presents themselves as a "notario," "notario público," "immigration consultant" or "preparer" and charges to handle a removal case. This site's [home-page warning about notarios](/en/#beware-of-notarios) covers the same point.

Other warning signs, consistent with what official agencies ask you to verify:

- They promise a **guaranteed result** (that you will "win," or that they have "contacts" in the government). No honest professional can guarantee the outcome of a removal case.
- They refuse to give a **bar number** or the state of licensure, or the name does not appear in the official directory.
- They demand **cash only**, with no receipt, or they ask you to sign blank forms.
- They tell you not to consult anyone else, or they keep your **original documents**.

If a relative is detained, first locate them with the guide on the [ICE detainee locator](/en/find-someone-detained-by-ice/) and, before you pay anyone who offers to "fix" the case, run these checks.

## What should I do if I was scammed?

Do not skip your next hearing or ignore case deadlines: reporting a scam is not a substitute for your defense in court. According to [USCIS](https://www.uscis.gov/scams-fraud-and-misconduct/avoid-scams/report-immigration-scams), **reporting a scam will not affect your immigration application or petition**.

USCIS points to these official channels:

- Report it to your [state consumer protection office](https://www.usa.gov/state-consumer).
- If you lost money or other property, report it to the **local police** as well. USA.gov explains [where to report scams](https://www.usa.gov/where-report-scams).
- You can always report it to the Federal Trade Commission at [ReportFraud.ftc.gov](https://reportfraud.ftc.gov/).

You may also:

- File a complaint with EOIR about an attorney, an accredited representative or the unauthorized practice of law, through [Submit a Complaint](https://www.justice.gov/eoir/submit-complaint) and, where it applies, the [Fraud and Abuse Prevention Program](https://www.justice.gov/eoir/filing-complaint-eoir-fraud-and-abuse-prevention-program). [Form EOIR-44](https://www.justice.gov/eoir/file/eoir44/dl) is the official complaint form against an immigration practitioner or organization.
- File a complaint with the **state bar** that issued the license, using the same official directory where you checked (or tried to check) the person.

Keep contracts, receipts, messages and the name the person used. Do not sign anything else until you have spoken with a licensed attorney or an accredited representative you have verified.

## Frequently asked questions

### How do I check whether someone is really a lawyer?

Ask for their full name and bar number, and look them up in the official directory of the state bar where they say they are licensed. Confirm that their license status allows them to practice now.

### What does "good standing" mean?

Each bar uses its own labels (for example, eligible to practice, active, or member in good standing). What matters is that the person is currently authorized to practice, and is not suspended or disbarred.

### Can an EOIR-accredited representative handle my court case?

Only if they are fully accredited and they act through the EOIR-recognized organization that sponsors them. A partially accredited representative may appear only before USCIS, not before the immigration court or the BIA.

### Where can I see whether EOIR has disciplined a lawyer or representative?

On EOIR's official list of currently disciplined practitioners. EOIR also publishes a list of people who were disciplined in the past and explains how to file a complaint.

### Can a notario represent me in a deportation case?

No. In the United States a notary public is not a lawyer and cannot represent you in immigration matters. Only a licensed attorney or an EOIR-accredited representative, on the terms EOIR describes, may do so.

### What should I do if I was scammed?

USCIS says you can report it to your state consumer protection office, to local police if you lost money, and always to the Federal Trade Commission. You can also file a complaint with EOIR or the state bar. USCIS states that reporting a scam will not affect your immigration application or petition.
