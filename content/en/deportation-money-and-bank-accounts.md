A deportation does not, by itself, confiscate a U.S. bank-account balance: I found no IRS, FDIC, or CFPB rule that says so. You may be able to keep the account, but access, tax withholding, and a departing-alien clearance can still apply. FDIC insurance does not require citizenship; it covers a bank failure, not a deportation.

## What happens to bank accounts?

I do not cite an official rule stating that a deportation, by itself, confiscates a bank-account balance: I did not find an IRS, FDIC, or CFPB page that says so. What those agencies do publish is narrower.

The [FDIC](https://www.fdic.gov/resources/deposit-insurance/brochures/insured-deposits) states that any person or entity can have deposit insurance at an insured bank, and that a person does not have to be a U.S. citizen or resident to have deposits insured by the FDIC. That insurance covers the balance, dollar for dollar, if the insured bank fails, up to the standard maximum of $250,000 per depositor, per insured bank, for each account ownership category. It does not guarantee everyday access to the account, and it does not describe what happens when someone leaves the country under a removal order.

The [CFPB](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-is-a-power-of-attorney-poa-en-1149/) explains that a power of attorney is a legal document that allows someone else to act on your behalf. A financial power of attorney can be used, for example, so that another person can pay bills or handle business during an absence. The CFPB also warns about the risk of abuse and suggests appointing only someone you trust and, if possible, getting help from a lawyer. A bank may have its own requirements for accepting the document. That does not, by itself, answer whether an account stays open after a deportation.

## What happens to a 401(k) or IRA?

The IRS treats retirement-plan distributions paid to a foreign person as a withholding issue. On its page about [plan distributions to foreign persons](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/plan-distributions-to-foreign-persons-require-withholding), the IRS states that you must generally withhold 30% of a distribution paid to a foreign payee for federal income tax under Internal Revenue Code section 1441(a), unless the payment can be reliably associated with valid documentation that establishes the payee is a U.S. person or a foreign person entitled to a rate lower than 30%. Documentation can include Form W-9, Form W-8BEN, or other appropriate sources.

On [pensions and annuity withholding](https://www.irs.gov/individuals/international-taxpayers/pensions-and-annuity-withholding), the IRS adds that distributions to nonresident aliens are generally subject to withholding under section 1441 unless a tax-treaty withholding exemption applies, and that withholding agents should not rely on a Form W-4P or Form W-4R received from a nonresident alien.

[Form W-8BEN](https://www.irs.gov/forms-pubs/about-form-w-8-ben) is the Certificate of Foreign Status of Beneficial Owner for United States Tax Withholding and Reporting (Individuals). The [official instructions](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/iw8ben.pdf) explain that foreign persons are generally subject to U.S. tax at a 30% rate on certain U.S.-source income, generally collected by withholding under section 1441, and that the form is used to establish that you are not a U.S. person, that you are the beneficial owner, and, if applicable, to claim a reduced rate or a treaty exemption. It must be provided to the withholding agent or payer before income is paid or credited. Failure to provide a Form W-8BEN when requested may lead to withholding at the foreign-person rate.

An additional tax can also apply if the withdrawal is early. The IRS states that amounts an individual withdraws from an IRA or retirement plan before reaching age 59½ are generally called early or premature distributions, and that an [additional 10% tax](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/plan-participant-employee/retirement-topics-exceptions-to-tax-on-early-distributions) must be paid unless an exception applies. [Topic no. 557](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc557) states, for traditional and Roth IRAs, that the additional 10% applies to the part of the distribution that must be included in gross income and is in addition to regular income tax. The IRS publishes lists of exceptions (for example, death or total and permanent disability). Deportation does not appear on those lists as an exception.

These rules do not mean the IRS keeps the entire balance because of a removal. They can, however, substantially reduce what you receive if a distribution is paid to a person treated as foreign. The result depends on your tax status, the documentation, and any treaty: a tax professional should review that.

## What taxes and forms can apply when you leave?

The IRS generally divides a [nonresident alien's](https://www.irs.gov/individuals/international-taxpayers/taxation-of-nonresident-aliens) U.S.-taxable income into two categories: income effectively connected with a U.S. trade or business, which, after allowable deductions, is taxed at graduated rates; and U.S.-source fixed, determinable, annual, or periodical (FDAP) income, which is taxed at a flat 30 percent (or a lower treaty rate, if you qualify), with no deductions against that income. The return the IRS identifies for a nonresident alien who must file is Form 1040-NR. Whether you are a resident or a nonresident for tax purposes is a different question from immigration status; it is covered in [Publication 519](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p519) and should be analyzed by a tax professional.

Before a planned long-term or permanent departure, the IRS also describes the [departing-alien clearance](https://www.irs.gov/individuals/international-taxpayers/departing-alien-clearance-sailing-permit), or sailing permit. According to that page, it is a document most aliens must secure from the IRS before leaving the United States, as proof that U.S. tax liabilities have been settled, after filing Form 1040-C or Form 2063, as applicable. The IRS lists specific exceptions (for example, certain diplomatic representatives, some students or exchange visitors, or certain short pleasure or business trips). Removal or deportation is not on that list. If you do not fall into one of those categories, the IRS states that you must obtain the permit and, on Form 1040-C, generally report income received or reasonably expected to be received through the departure date and pay the tax due, except in the situations the IRS itself describes.

The IRS frames that process around a planned departure, with an appointment at a local office. I did not find a specific instruction on that page for someone leaving under a removal order. For that reason I do not state that every person who is deported can complete a sailing permit, or that they are exempt.

The IRS also notes that lawful permanent residents who have no definite plans to return and who plan to surrender their green card may, if they have been lawful permanent residents in at least 8 taxable years during the previous 15, including the year they surrender the card, have to notify the Department of Homeland Security of the end of residency and file Form 8854. The details are in Publication 519. That is not a rule that is triggered by removal alone.

## Is illegal entry a crime or a felony?

People often call it "illegal entry." [8 U.S.C. 1325](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-2024-title8/html/USCODE-2024-title8-chap12-subchapII-partVIII-sec1325.htm) is titled "Improper entry by alien." Subsection (a) covers any alien who (1) enters or attempts to enter the United States at a time or place other than as designated by immigration officers, (2) eludes examination or inspection, or (3) attempts to enter or obtains entry by a willfully false or misleading representation or the willful concealment of a material fact. For the first commission of any such offense, the statute provides a fine under title 18, imprisonment of not more than 6 months, or both. For a subsequent commission, it provides a fine under title 18, imprisonment of not more than 2 years, or both.

That text does not use the words "misdemeanor" or "felony." The general classification is in [18 U.S.C. 3559](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-2024-title18/html/USCODE-2024-title18-partII-chap227-subchapA-sec3559.htm): a maximum of 6 months or less but more than 30 days is a Class B misdemeanor; a maximum of less than 5 years but more than 1 year is a Class E felony. A first 1325(a) offense is therefore a Class B misdemeanor, and a subsequent offense is a Class E felony. Section 1325(b) adds a civil penalty of at least $50 and not more than $250 for each entry or attempted entry at an undesignated time or place, or twice that amount if the person was previously subject to that civil penalty, in addition to criminal penalties.

Reentry after removal is in [8 U.S.C. 1326](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-2024-title8/html/USCODE-2024-title8-chap12-subchapII-partVIII-sec1326.htm). Subsection (a) applies, subject to the exceptions in the statute itself (for example, if the Attorney General has expressly consented to a new application for admission), to an alien who has been denied admission, excluded, deported, or removed, or who departed while an order was outstanding, and who thereafter enters, attempts to enter, or is found in the United States. The general penalty is a fine under title 18, imprisonment of not more than 2 years, or both: a Class E felony under 18 U.S.C. 3559(a)(5). Subsection (b) raises the maximum in certain cases, to not more than 10 years, or to not more than 20 years if the removal was subsequent to a conviction for an aggravated felony.

These criminal offenses are separate from the civil removal process in [immigration court](/en/immigration-court/). An NTA is not, by itself, a conviction under 1325 or 1326.

| Subsequent improper entry (8 U.S.C. 1325(a)) | Fine under title 18, imprisonment of not more than 2 years, or both | Class E felony (3559(a)(5)) |

## Frequently asked questions

### What happens to my bank account if I get deported?

I did not find an official IRS, FDIC, or CFPB rule stating that a deportation confiscates the balance. Whether you can keep and use the account depends on the bank's contract and on tax rules that may apply after you leave.

### Can I keep my bank account if I get deported?

There is no official rule I found that forces the account to close solely because of a removal order. Banks set their own terms; FDIC insurance, if the bank is insured, does not require U.S. citizenship or residency and covers a bank failure, not a deportation.

### Does the IRS withhold 30% if a nonresident takes a 401(k) or IRA distribution?

Generally, yes. The IRS states that 30% is generally withheld from a plan distribution paid to a foreign payee under Internal Revenue Code section 1441(a), unless valid documentation establishes that the payee is a U.S. person or is entitled to a lower rate, for example on Form W-8BEN.

### What is Form W-8BEN?

It is the Certificate of Foreign Status of Beneficial Owner for United States Tax Withholding and Reporting (Individuals). The IRS uses it to establish that you are not a U.S. person and, if applicable, to claim a reduced rate or a treaty exemption. It must be given to the withholding agent before you are paid.

### Is there an extra tax if I withdraw retirement money before age 59½?

Generally, yes. The IRS states that amounts withdrawn from an IRA or retirement plan before age 59½ are early distributions and that an additional 10% tax applies unless an exception applies. That 10% is on top of regular income tax.

### What is a sailing permit or Form 1040-C?

The IRS calls a departing-alien clearance, or sailing permit, the document most aliens must obtain before a planned long-term or permanent departure. It is issued after you file Form 1040-C or Form 2063, as applicable, and is proof that U.S. tax liabilities have been settled. The IRS lists specific exceptions.

### Does FDIC insurance require U.S. citizenship or residency?

No. The FDIC states that any person or entity can have coverage at an insured bank and that a person does not have to be a U.S. citizen or resident for deposits to be insured. That coverage applies if the insured bank fails, up to the standard limit, not because of a deportation.

### Is illegal entry a crime or a felony?

Improper entry is a federal crime under 8 U.S.C. 1325(a): a first offense may be punished by a fine or imprisonment of not more than 6 months, or both. Under 18 U.S.C. 3559(a)(7), that maximum classifies the offense as a Class B misdemeanor. Reentry after removal under 8 U.S.C. 1326(a) may be punished by a fine or imprisonment of not more than 2 years, or both; 18 U.S.C. 3559(a)(5) classifies that maximum as a Class E felony. Higher maxima apply to a subsequent 1325(a) offense and under 1326(b).
