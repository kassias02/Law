This page describes rights that apply to everyone in the United States regardless of immigration status. It is not legal advice about your case.

## If they knock

**Do not open immediately.** You can speak through a closed door. Opening the door is sometimes treated as allowing entry.

**Ask who they are and what they want.** You may ask them to identify themselves and state the reason.

**Ask to see the warrant.** This is the decisive part:

- A **judicial search warrant** names a court, usually "U.S. District Court", names you and your specific address, and is **signed by a judge**. That does authorize entry.
- An **ICE administrative warrant**, Form **I-200** (arrest) or **I-205** (removal), is signed by an immigration officer, not a judge. It **does not authorize entry into your home** without consent.

Ask them to slide it under the door or hold it to a window. Read which court it names and who signed it.

**If there is no judicial warrant, you can say no.** One clear sentence is enough: "I do not consent to your entry."

## If they come in anyway

Do not resist physically and do not argue. Say clearly: "I do not consent to this search." That matters later, when a lawyer reviews whether what happened was lawful.

Do not lie and do not present false documents — that creates a new and serious criminal problem. Staying silent is legal; lying is not.

## What to say and what not to

You can say: **"I want to remain silent. I want to speak to a lawyer."** Repeat it as often as needed.

You are not required to answer questions about:

- Your country of birth.
- How or when you entered the United States.
- Your immigration status.

Do not sign **anything** without a lawyer reading it. A document may waive your right to see a judge or accept voluntary departure. If you are pressured with the line that signing "speeds things up", that is precisely the reason not to sign.

If you have documents proving lawful status, you may show them. Never show foreign documents that reveal information you would rather not give.

## At work

ICE cannot enter private areas of a business without a judicial warrant or the owner's permission. Public areas are different.

You keep the right to remain silent and not to sign. Do not run: fleeing can be used against you and is dangerous.

## On the street or in a car

If stopped, ask whether you are being detained or free to go. If free to go, leave calmly. If detained, stay silent and ask for a lawyer. At a traffic stop the driver must produce a license and registration; passengers need not answer questions about status.

## Make the plan today, not on the day

This is what actually changes outcomes:

- **A lawyer's number** memorized or written in each adult's wallet. In detention you will not have your phone.
- **A trusted person** designated in writing to collect the children, with authorization on file at the school.
- **Documents gathered and copied**: passports, children's birth certificates, proof of address, tax returns, medical records. Leave copies with someone you trust.
- **Every family member's A-Number**, written down somewhere outside the house.
- **A power of attorney** for decisions about children and finances, prepared with a lawyer. For official IRS and FDIC rules on [what happens to your money and bank accounts if you are deported](/en/deportation-money-and-bank-accounts/), see that guide.

If a family member is detained, see [what happens after an ICE arrest](/en/what-happens-after-an-ice-arrest/). ## Frequently asked questions

### Do I have to open the door for ICE?

No, unless they have a search warrant signed by a judge naming you and your address. Ask them to slide it under the door or show it through a window first.

### How do I tell a judicial warrant from an administrative one?

A judicial warrant names a court such as "U.S. District Court" and is signed by a judge. ICE administrative warrants (I-200, I-205) are signed by an immigration officer and do not authorize home entry.

### What if I sign papers without a lawyer?

You may be waiving your right to see a judge or accepting voluntary departure. Some of those documents are very hard to undo.

### Can ICE just stop you and ask for ID?

An immigration officer may ask questions as long as the officer does not restrain your freedom to walk away, under 8 C.F.R. 287.8(b)(1). A brief detention for questioning requires reasonable suspicion, based on specific articulable facts, that you are engaged in an offense against the United States or are an alien illegally in the United States (8 C.F.R. 287.8(b)(2)). 8 U.S.C. 1357(a)(1) authorizes an officer, without a warrant, to interrogate any alien or person believed to be an alien as to the right to be or remain in the United States. The Fourth Amendment protects against unreasonable searches and seizures, and the Fifth Amendment includes the right to remain silent. A judicial warrant is signed by a judge; an ICE administrative warrant (I-200 or I-205) is signed by an immigration officer and does not authorize entry into a home without consent.

### Can ICE pull you over or detain you if you are a U.S. citizen?

ICE Directive 16001.2 states that, as a matter of law, ICE cannot assert its civil immigration enforcement authority to arrest and/or detain a U.S. citizen. That does not prevent an officer from asking questions if you are free to leave, as 8 C.F.R. 287.8(b)(1) distinguishes. A brief detention for questioning under 8 C.F.R. 287.8(b)(2) requires reasonable suspicion that the person is engaged in an offense against the United States or is an alien illegally in the United States.

### Can ICE deport a U.S. citizen?

Civil removal authority applies to aliens, not to U.S. citizens. 8 U.S.C. 1101(a)(3) defines "alien" as any person not a citizen or national of the United States, and 8 U.S.C. 1227 describes classes of deportable aliens. ICE Directive 16001.2 states that, as a matter of law, ICE cannot assert its civil immigration enforcement authority to arrest and/or detain a U.S. citizen.
